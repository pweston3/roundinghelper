How to round numbers

The same four steps work for every place, from the nearest ten to the nearest hundred thousand, and for decimals too.

The four steps

Take 4,730 to the nearest hundred.

  1. Find the place. The hundreds digit is the 7: 4,730
  2. Find the two round numbers it sits between. Keep the 7 and everything left of it, turn the rest to zeros, and that is the lower one. The upper one is one hundred more: 4,700 and 4,800
  3. Look at the digit just to the right. That single digit decides it. Here it is the 3: 4,730
  4. Pick the closer one. 3 is less than 5, so the 7 stays and everything after it becomes a zero. 4,730 → 4,700

Step 2 is the one usually skipped, and it is the one that makes the rest make sense. Once a child can see that the answer has to be either 4,700 or 4,800, step 4 is a question about distance rather than a rule to remember.

When the deciding digit is exactly 5

Then the number sits exactly halfway and neither neighbor is closer. 4,750 is fifty away from 4,700 and fifty away from 4,800.

The convention taught in school is to go up, so 4,750 becomes 4,800. Worth saying out loud to a child that this one is a rule people agreed on rather than something they can work out, because a child who is genuinely reasoning about distance will otherwise be confused by it, and reasonably so.

Rounding decimals

Nothing changes. To round 3.47 to the nearest tenth, the tenths digit is the 4, the two neighbors are 3.4 and 3.5, and the deciding digit is the 7. Seven is 5 or more, so the 4 goes up: 3.47 → 3.5

The one thing that trips people up is which direction to count from the decimal point. Tenths, hundredths and thousandths are to the right of it. Ones, tens and hundreds are to the left.

The mistake most children make

They round every question to the same place.

It is not carelessness. A worksheet normally asks for one place all the way down the page, so after the first question there is no reason to read the instruction again, and a child who stops reading it still scores full marks. The habit is invisible until the place changes between questions, and then it looks like they have forgotten how to round, when what they have actually learned is that the instruction is safe to ignore.

The fix is mixing the places rather than doing more questions. A child who has to find the place fresh every time cannot answer on autopilot.

Why this site says "went up" and "stayed"

Ordinary usage is "round up" and "round down", and that is what most people search for. The practice here deliberately avoids both.

"Round down" suggests the number gets smaller in some visible way, and children respond by changing the digit. Rounding down does not change it at all: the digit stays exactly as it was and only the digits after it become zeros. Saying "it stayed" describes what actually happens to the digit in front of them, and one common error disappears with it.
